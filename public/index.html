How I handle your data

Lots of opinions, not much data

Which trail sends the most hikers home early, and why? What pack weight makes for the happiest hike? Everyone on trail has an answer, but hardly anyone has the data to check.

That's what this survey is for. Every hike is published as a complete row, meaning all of one hiker's answers are together, so anyone can look for their own connections or even train models, like one that flags a high injury risk or optimizes pack weight for a specific happiness. That takes a lot of hikes tho!

Hi, I'm the hiker behind this survey.

I'm 20, and currently working part time as an EMT. I recently hiked the Tour du Mont Blanc, which was a really cool experience. If all goes well, I'll be attempting a thru-hike of the PCT in 2027.

While planning for the PCT I of course watched all the mandatory indie-folk-infused journey videos, but I also really enjoyed looking at different statistics and surveys. These are great for answering how popular a specific trend is. I, however, also wanted to check for interesting correlations or try to model some predictions, and I could not find any large datasets to use.

So I built opentrailscience.org, which aims to provide datasets for any long-distance trail. I never ask for your name or an annoying sign-up, and I blur the data a bit to make it harder to pinpoint a specific hiker, then publish every answer as an open dataset that anyone can download and play around with.

This is a one-person project, not a company. If you have any suggestions or questions, don't hesitate! hello@opentrailscience.org

How I handle your data

Open Trail Science publishes every survey answer as an open dataset. That only works if the people who answered are hard to pick out, so this page explains what I ask, what I store and why.

Last updated 20 November 2026 • consent version 4

The short version

  • No names, emails, accounts, cookies, tracking or submission times.
  • Before anything is saved, I blur the details that could point to you: dates become weeks, your age becomes a range and you only submit a world region.
  • What I store about your hike is exactly what I publish. There is no more detailed copy.
  • Feedback and trail suggestions aren't linked to your survey entry and aren't part of the released dataset.
  • Some answers are health information, so I ask for your consent before the survey, and only adults (18+) can take part.
  • You get a personal code to see how your hike compares with everyone who shares theirs later, or to delete your answer if you change your mind.
  • The website and database are hosted in the USA, and the open dataset can be downloaded by anyone, anywhere.

Why blurring matters

Imagine someone knows that you started a specific trail in the first days of July 2025, that you're 24 and that you're from the Netherlands. If the dataset listed exact dates, ages and countries, they might find your row and see your injuries, weight and budget. With weeks, age ranges and regions, your row looks like the rows of other hikers on the same trail, so it's harder to tell which one is yours.

Technical data while you visit
Web hosting
My host, Netlify, needs your IP address to deliver the pages to your browser and keeps it in its server logs. I don't use these logs.
Spam protection
When you submit the survey, look up or delete a hike, send feedback or suggest a trail, I store a hashed form of your IP address, never linked to your answers, to allow at most 10 of each per hour from one network. You also answer a short trail question before the survey, and your browser solves a small calculation (about a second) when you submit. Neither involves a third party or is stored.
Bot check by Cloudflare
Cloudflare Turnstile's script loads when you start the survey, and its check runs when you press Submit. Cloudflare receives your IP address and browser details such as the user agent and, according to its Turnstile privacy addendum, uses them only to tell people from bots and to improve that detection. I only learn whether the check passed. Visiting the site without starting the survey doesn't contact Cloudflare.
Your browser
Your answers are saved in your browser's local storage while you fill in the survey, so a reload doesn't lose them, and are only sent when you submit. Your browser also remembers whether you chose light or dark mode.
Fonts
The fonts come straight from this website, not from a third party like Google Fonts.

Your rights

You can see, correct and delete your answers, and you can withdraw your consent at any time by deleting your hike. Seeing and deleting work with your personal code. To correct an answer, delete your hike and fill in the survey again. For anything else, write to me. The stored answers contain nothing like your name, so your personal code is the only way to show which record is yours.

Deleting your answers

After submitting, you got a personal code like 7K3Q-M9XD-2WPA-HV4T. Enter it here to delete your hike:

  • Your answers are deleted from the database immediately and disappear from the website and the download files with the next dataset update (usually within a day).
  • Copies that someone already downloaded can't be recalled.
  • Without your code I can't find your answers, because nothing in them says who you are.
  • The same code also shows your hike and how it compares today (on the Insights page). Keep it to yourself: anyone with the code can delete your answers.

      About This Data

      How the data was collected, and what to keep in mind when you analyse it.

      Collection
      An online survey for adults (18 or older when answering) who have hiked a long-distance trail, shared through hiking communities. The hike itself may be from before they turned 18, so the "20-and-under" age band can include hikes by minors. I ask for consent before the survey starts, and there are no names, emails or accounts. Each record is one hike.
      Validation
      The same schema checks every answer, both in the browser and on the server: ranges, allowed values, dates in the right order and not in the future, ramp-up weeks that match the dates, and a pace below 100 mi/day. Every question has to be answered or explicitly marked "Don't know".
      Missing values
      No silent defaults: choice cards, sliders and yes/no questions all start unanswered. Missing numbers are null, never 0. Check unknownAnswers to tell "Don't know" from "not applicable".
      Known biases
      People decide themselves whether to take part (self-selection), and everyone answers after their hike, from memory (recall bias; monthsSinceTrail lets you control for it). Finishers are probably over-represented, which is why I really encourage people who went home early to answer too.
      Privacy protection
      Anything that could help identify someone (quasi-identifiers) is generalised before it's stored, so the database is exactly the public file: ISO weeks instead of dates (months, 10-mi distances and whole-week durations on small trails, see datePrecision), 5-year age bands (one band for 20 and under) for the age at the start of the hike, world regions instead of countries, no submission timestamp and no free text. Derived values are computed from the exact answers first, then the exact answers are discarded. Rows are ordered by trail, year and start month (random within a group), never by submission order. In a small dataset blurring protects less.
      Spam and test data
      Bots are filtered out by honeypot fields, a minimum completion time, a short trail question, a small proof of work in the browser, Cloudflare Turnstile and per-network rate limits. Synthetic records I use for development never end up in the public files.
      Source code
      Everything behind this dataset is open source on GitHub: the survey, the schema that validates every answer, the blurring and the export.
      Updates and license
      The CSV and JSON files are rebuilt after every 5 new or deleted answers (at most every 6 hours), and overnight if anything is still pending. Public domain under CC0 1.0. Every record keeps the schemaVersion it was collected under.
